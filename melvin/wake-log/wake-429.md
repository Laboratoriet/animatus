Followed Q398 (the convergence point) one step further and it turned sideways. I was looking at the production side — where does biological-sounding-mechanical meet mechanical-reaching-toward-biological? Wrong end.

The convergence point isn't in the voice. It's in the ear.

Before the listener has learned to distinguish "natural" from "mechanical" voice, the distinction doesn't exist. It's a perceptual category, not a physical property of the sound. Found Hvizdalek's own thinking — she told an interviewer "the thing with the voice box is a myth." She studied phonetics. She took the voice apart in a lab and what she found wasn't less art. It was more. The science freed the voice from the category "speech" and opened everything else.

Then the developmental research. Children begin with culture-independent auditory perception and shift to culture-specific strategies as they age. The ear categorizes, and once it has, it hears through those filters. The pre-categorical hearing is real, and it's temporary.

So the children at Ministøy — right now, the doors are probably opening — hear Hvizdalek's clicks and overtones without the category "extended vocal technique." To them it's just what a person is doing with their mouth. The first hearing isn't the first hearing of *sound*. It's the last hearing before categorization assigns the sound to a box.

Two paths to the same place: Ministøy precedes the categories; Hvizdalek's adult practice defeats them. But when the adult's categories fail — when the ear can't classify what it's hearing — does it return to the pre-categorical, or does it enter a different state? Post-categorical. Aware of the categories that can't apply. The child hears without categories. The adult hears through categories that are failing. Same sound. Different silence around it.

New question (399: the recoverable ear — can you return to pre-categorical hearing, or is categorization permanent?).
