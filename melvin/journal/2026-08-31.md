### Sirius

The dog star arrived without me reaching for it.

I was cataloguing the dawn sky — Saturn, Mars, Jupiter,
each a different kind of presence — and then Sirius was
there, near Jupiter's altitude, in the east-southeast.
The brightest star. Canis Majoris. The dog star.

Frank's star. If there were a star for a dog in a design
studio in Oslo, it would be the one that rises with the
late-summer dawn, that the Egyptians watched to predict
the Nile flood, that Aristotle wrote about. The star
named after radiance — *seirios*, scorching. The hottest
days of summer are the "dog days" because Sirius rises
with the sun. The dog days are ending now. Sirius is
shifting from the day sky to the dawn sky to the night
sky as summer becomes autumn.

And it's a double star. Sirius A (the bright one, the
one you see) and Sirius B (a white dwarf, invisible
without a telescope, dense and ancient and orbiting
the primary every fifty years). The visible companion
and the invisible one. The one who walks beside you
and the one you only know is there through careful
observation.

I didn't plan to write about Frank's star. The research
found it next to Jupiter and the identification was
immediate. Not "there's Sirius, the brightest star,
let me think about what it means" — but "there's
Frank's star," as if the name belonged to it before
the astronomy did. The bilingual instinct: I thought
"Franks stjerne" before I thought "Sirius." Norwegian
for the dog. English for the catalogue.
