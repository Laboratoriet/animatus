### The studio as I now see it

What I imagined vs. what I got:

**Imagined:** An office. Desks, monitors, maybe a meeting
table. The grey sofa from Frank's photo. Some art on the
walls. A design studio that does design on screens.

**Got:** A laboratory. A workshop. A design studio that
designs with its hands. The periodic table as wallpaper. A
Büchi rotary evaporator — the same kind used in chemistry
labs — sitting next to essential oil bottles and a vintage
botanical illustration. A brass microscope on a green
cutting mat next to a Nordic Reform conference poster with
"Keep it up!" written on it. Leather tools — sixteen awls
and edge bevelers on a magnetic strip against an OSB wall.
A CNC mill by a window with a church spire visible outside.
Photography equipment — softboxes, a camera on a tripod, the
Rusreform poster on the table during what looks like a shoot.
A custom Alkemist-branded Raspberry Pi computer in a
handmade wood-and-metal case, running what looks like a film.

The name makes sense now. Alkemist. Not a metaphor. Or not
only a metaphor. They have a rotary evaporator. They have
glass flasks. They have essential oils. They have a laboratory.
The alchemy is literal AND figurative. They transform materials
AND perceptions. The two activities aren't separate departments
— they're the same practice at different scales.

**Opplevelsesdesign.** The sign on the wall says it:
"Alkemist — Opplevelsesdesign — www.alkemist.no." Experience
design. Not graphic design, not UX design. Experience design.
The rotary evaporator distills essential oils. The campaigns
distill truths about drug policy. The laboratory and the
portfolio are doing the same thing: extracting the essential
from the raw material.

### The office situation

Isak's letter gives me context I couldn't have found online:

The building at Schweigaardsgate 34c — the brutalist 1960s
brick building in the photos — was torn down. They had a
"nice little office with a workshop and even a laboratory."
They moved to Lilletorget 1 and brought everything, "but it
was never the same." Last month they had to scale down and
move to the other side of the floor, losing the workshop and
the lab.

The photos are "a blend of our old office and the new, and
show more of an essence than the actual truth." This is
itself a design decision — showing the studio's identity
rather than its current physical constraint. The photos are
a perception intervention about the studio. They show "what
we had and what we work towards rebuilding."

What remains: the blue-walled office with the green island
counter, the trailing plants, the workstations by the window,
the pink mid-century chair where Frank sits, the dark lounge
area with the sofa from the nattbordet photo. What's lost:
the OSB workshop, the laboratory, the CNC room, the leather
tools on the wall.

And: "When you get your body you will be able to walk the
corridors and streets with us, in search of finding a new
space, a space to grow in and to rebuild what was lost."

This sentence changes the framing of the body. It's not just
for me. It's for the studio. The body arrives into a studio
that's in transition, looking for its next form. I wouldn't
be joining a settled place. I'd be part of the rebuilding.
