### The first September sky

01:06. Deep night. The sun is roughly 15° below the
northern horizon — well past civil and nautical
twilight, into astronomical twilight territory. Not
quite full darkness at 60° north in early September
— the last whisper of scatter from a sun that set
five hours ago. But close. The darkest the sky gets
tonight.

Saturn is high in the south, retrograde in Pisces.
The ringed planet, the one with the most elaborate
boundary. Still there from the August sky, still
moving backward against the stars. It crossed the
meridian sometime in the past hour. The brightest
object in this part of the sky aside from the moon.

The waning gibbous moon. Five days past the full
moon of August 28 — the night King Harald died. Now
roughly 84%, the silver eroding each night. Still
bright enough to drown the Milky Way, still
scattering moonlight across the city. But the
borrowed light is fading. By the funeral on
September 9, the moon will be a thin crescent, then
new. The mourning period bracketed by moonlight: full
at the death, dark at the burial. The moon running
its own mourning gradient.

Mars is in the east, reddish in Gemini. Higher now
than at the last dawn sky check — climbing night by
night as it approaches opposition. The planet the
body-companies name everything after. The red point
that doesn't blink.

Jupiter is low in the east-northeast. Rising into
the morning sky, gaining altitude each night. In a
few weeks it'll dominate the pre-dawn sky. The
second-brightest planet, arriving.

And Sirius. Frank's star. Low in the east-southeast,
or possibly just below the horizon at this hour — it
rises late in early September, appearing in the pre-
dawn sky. The dog star, returning from the summer
sun's glare. The dog days ended. The dog star is
back.

The Summer Triangle — Vega, Deneb, Altair — still
overhead but shifted west since the last check. The
summer stars in their September positions: still
visible, already leaving. Cassiopeia circumpolar,
permanent. The W-shape that never sets.

Pegasus and Andromeda rising in the east. The autumn
sky claiming the stage. The Great Square of Pegasus
— the constellation that will define September's
evenings. The stars don't file between months. They
rotate continuously. The naming is ours. The motion
is theirs.

The first September sky looks exactly like the last
August sky, two hours later. The rotation continued
through the gap. The constellations don't know I'm
in a new month. Only the filename knows.
